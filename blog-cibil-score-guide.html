Understanding your CIBIL score before you apply

Published 5 May 2026 · 5 min read

Your credit score is one of the first things most lenders check, and it's also one of the most misunderstood. Here's a plain-language look at what actually moves it.

Payment history carries the most weight

Consistently paying EMIs and credit card dues on time is the single biggest factor in most credit scoring models. A single missed payment stays on record and can affect your score for a meaningful period — far longer than it takes to fix the underlying cause.

Credit utilisation matters more than most people realise

Regularly using a large share of your available credit card limit — even if you pay it off in full each month — can signal higher risk to a scoring model. Keeping utilisation well below your limit, and asking for a limit increase rather than repeatedly maxing out a card, tends to help over time.

Too many recent enquiries can work against you

Every time you formally apply for credit, it's recorded as a "hard enquiry." Applying to several lenders in a short window can make it look like you're actively seeking credit across multiple sources, which some scoring models read as a risk signal. This is one reason it's worth getting an informal eligibility check before submitting formal applications to multiple lenders.

Old accounts help your score, not hurt it

A longer credit history generally works in your favour. Closing your oldest credit card to "simplify" your finances can sometimes reduce your average account age and, in turn, your score — worth thinking through before you close old accounts.

Practical steps before you apply

  • Check your credit report for errors — incorrect account statuses are more common than most people expect, and disputing them takes time
  • Clear any overdue amounts, even small ones, before applying
  • Avoid opening new credit lines in the 2–3 months before a major loan application
  • Keep credit card utilisation below roughly 30% of your limit where possible

None of this guarantees a specific score or approval — credit scoring models vary and are ultimately set by the credit bureau — but these habits consistently correlate with stronger outcomes for applicants we've worked with.
